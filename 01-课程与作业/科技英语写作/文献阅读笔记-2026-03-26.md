Liquid lead-bismuth eutectic (LBE) has many attractive properties including low neutron absorption, good gamma shielding, low melting point (123.5 ◦C) and high boiling point (1670 ◦C), high thermal conductivity, and low chemical reactivity. These properties make LBE a promising coolant candidate for the fast neutron nuclear reactors [1]. However, the corrosion degradation of steels exposed to liquid LBE limits the deployment of many LBE-cooled reactor systems [2]. According to the American Society of Mechanical Engineers Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code in Division 5 of Section III, five alloys are currently qualified for use in high-temperature nuclear components: 2.25Cr-1Mo and 9Cr-1Mo ferritic steels, Types 304 and 316 stainless steels, and Alloy 800 H. Fe-Cr-Ni austenitic stainless (AuS) steels and Fe-Cr ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steels with compositions similar to the code-qualified Type 316 and 9Cr-1Mo-V steels are the two best candidates for LBE-cooled reactors. The austenite stabilizing element Ni in the AuS steel is more prone to the dissolution in liquid LBE due to the high solubilities of Ni in liquid LBE, e.g., solubility of Ni at 550 ◦C is 3.2 wt% complementary to the values of ~16 wppm for Cr and ~5 wppm for Fe in LBE [3]. Compared to AuS steels, F/M steels exhibit improved dissolution resistance, but are inherently susceptible to the LBE induced liquid metal embrittlement (LME) [2,4].

In order to mitigate the Ni dissolution and LME, the current strategy is to form a protective oxide scale on the steel surface by controlling the concentration of dissolved oxygen in the liquid LBE within specific ranges [5]. As motivated by this oxygen control strategy, a large body of studies were dedicated to investigate the oxidation behaviors of AuS and F/M steels exposed to oxygen-containing liquid LBE in the past two decades [6–17]. The structure of oxide scale formed on F/M steels has been studied more extensively, which consists of an outer magnetite (Fe3O4) layer and an inner Fe-Cr spinel (FexCr3-xO4) layer at moderate temperatures, and an internal oxidation zone (IOZ) underneath the spinel layer at temperatures above ~550 ◦C [7–14]. Based on the summarized oxidation mechanisms, kinetic models for the oxidation of Fe-Cr F/M steels in liquid LBE have been developed by Zhang [18–21] and Martinelli et al. [22–25], although the modeling of IOZ growth kinetics is still lacking. In comparison, AuS steels exhibit improved oxidation resistance than the F/M steels, which have been attributed to the higher Cr content in the AuS steel and therefore in the Fe-Cr spinel layer [6]. The oxide scale formed on AuS steels in LBE was believed to be composed of the outer magnetite layer and inner Fe-Cr spinel layer. However, the use of focused ion beam (FIB) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed more complex structure in the previously believed Fe-Cr spinel layer that includes a bi-phasic IOZ structure consisting of Ni-rich metal phase and Fe-Cr spinel phase [26,27]. It was suggested that the bi-phasic structure was due to the nonreactive nature of Ni and the faster diffusion kinetics of O and Fe, and ensuing segregations of Ni and Cr along preferentially oxidized grain boundaries (GBs) [27]. Moreover, preferential intergranular oxidation (PIO) was frequently observed on AuS steels with presence of Ni segregations on the grain boundaries (GBs) and the steel/oxide interface.

However, the time evolution of the Ni segregations and its influence on oxidation kinetics, particularly the PIO propagation, remains unclear. This is important to establish a mechanistic understanding of the oxidation process and to develop predictive models for evaluating the lifetime of the nuclear components. In respect of the oxide scale characterization, the use of synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-focusing and FIB-TEM to advance our understanding of the corrosion mechanisms in a wide variety of environments has been highlighted in recent studies [28–32]. Therefore, the present work uses a combination of state-of-the-art micro-to-atomic microscopy techniques, including secondary electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD), synchrotron radiation micro-X-ray diffraction (μ-XRD), and FIB-TEM, to investigate the structure evolution of the oxide scale formed on AuS 316L exposed to liquid LBE.

Lead-bismuth eutectic (LBE, Pb44.5Bi55.5, wt. %) has emerged as a primary candidate coolant and spallation target material for Generation IV lead-cooled fast reactors (LFRs) and accelerator-driven systems (ADS), owing to its exceptional neutron economy, thermohydraulic properties, and chemical stability at elevated temperatures [1–5]. The low melting point (123.5 ◦C), high boiling point (1670 ◦C) and low vapor pressure of LBE impart enhanced thermal efficiency and inherent safety margin to reactor when operating above 500 ◦C [1,3]. However, the practical implementation of LBE-cooled systems faces critical challenges in LBE-materials compatibility, particularly liquid metal corrosion (LMC) and liquid metal embrittlement (LME) of structural materials [1,2,5,6]. The primary alloying elements (Fe, Cr, Ni) of two candidate structural materials (ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steel and austenitic stainless steel (AuSS)) for LFRs exhibit high solubility in LBE, particularly the austenite-stabilizing element Ni, which demonstrates solubility several orders of magnitude higher than Fe and Cr [5,6]. Moreover, the solubility increases significantly with rising temperature [6]. Consequently, when unprotected steel surfaces are directly exposed to LBE under elevated operating temperatures (>450 ◦C for AuSSs and >550 ◦C for F/M steels), severe dissolution occurs [1]. Dissolution induces accelerated metal recession and austenite-to-ferrite phase transformation, progressively compromising structural integrity [7–11]. Furthermore, direct contact between LBE and structural materials may also induce LME, causing reduction in toughness or ductility [1,12,13]. The current strategy to mitigate dissolution corrosion and LME involves controlling the dissolved oxygen concentration in liquid LBE to form a protective oxide scale on the material surface, thereby isolating the metallic substrate from the corrosive LBE environment [14]. However, excessive oxide thickness not only compromises the load-bearing capacity of materials, but also significantly impairs the heat transfer efficiency of thermal exchange components such as fuel cladding and heat exchange tubes [1].

Compared to F/M steels, AuSSs exhibit superior high-temperature mechanical properties, enhanced oxidation resistance, and reduced susceptibility to LME, making them the main candidate structural materials for out-of-core components in LFRs [1,5,12,15]. However, the high solubility of Ni in LBE renders that AuSSs operate within oxygen-controlled environments [14]. Extensive investigations on the oxidation behavior of AuSSs in oxygen-containing LBE have been systematically conducted over recent decades [9,16–31]. It has been widely reported that AuSSs frequently exhibit preferential intergranular oxidation (PIO) in LBE [16–20,23,25,26,28–32]. PIO has also been extensively documented in AuSSs and Ni-based alloys exposed to high temperature water [33–38], high temperature steam [39–41], supercritical water (SCW) [42–44], and supercritical carbon dioxide (S-CO2) or high temperature CO2 [45–47], with increasing studies confirming the close correlation between PIO and stress corrosion cracking (SCC) in these environments [33,40,43,48–51]. As for LBE environments, the intergranular oxidation may also induce preferential cracking along oxidized grain boundaries (GBs). Investigation on low-cycle fatigue behavior of T91 steel in oxygen-saturated LBE (150–550 ◦C) by Xue et al. [52] revealed that enhanced oxidation at 550 ◦C degraded matrix strength while increasing crack initiation sites. Wang et al. [32] found that most cracks, formed on 316Ti steel under tensile stress in oxygen-containing LBE at 550 ◦C, stopped at the oxide/matrix interface, while deeper cracks propagating along oxidized GBs into the metal matrix. Notably, Wang et al. [25] observed pronounced intergranular cracking along preferentially oxidized GBs in 15–15Ti steel exposed to LBE at 550 ◦C without external stress, accompanied by Pb infiltration into the cracks. This indicates that dissolution induced by Pb penetration along oxidized GBs can directly lead to intergranular cracking. These preferentially oxidized GBs would serve as preferential cracking paths, triggering premature failure. Therefore, the degradation in loading capacity of AuSSs in LBE environments is closely related to the PIO behavior which should be carefully studied.

Several investigations have been conducted on the PIO behavior of austenitic steels in oxygen-containing LBE [19,23,25,28,29,31]. It has been revealed that the oxidized GBs are decorated with Fe-Cr spinel [28,29,31]. Nano-network structured IOZ (internal oxidation zone) was observed on the flanks of intergranular oxides [19,23,28,29,31]. Unoxidized metallic particles rich in Ni and depleted in Cr, termed Ni-enriched grains (NiEGs), were frequently observed either wrapped by intergranular oxides or on their periphery [23,25,28,29,31]. Nanoscale pores, which are believed to be spatially interconnected and act as the fast diffusion pathway during the oxidation process [19,26,27,53–59], were also distributed between intergranular oxides [19,23,28]. Additionally, some elongated pores were observed along the oxidized GBs [23,28]. Notably, the Ni-enriched and Cr-depleted diffusion induced grain boundary migration (DIGM) zones were observed at the PIO fronts in 15–15Ti steel exposed to oxygen-saturated LBE at 550 ◦C by Lu et al. [29]. These GB migration zones are induced by the selective rapid diffusion of alloying elements (e.g., Cr) along GBs, and are frequently observed at random high-angle grain boundaries (RHABs) of austenitic alloys in various corrosive environments at temperature below 0.6 Tm (melting temperature) where GB diffusion is dominant [33–37,39–41,44,47,60].

However, the PIO mechanism of AuSSs in oxygen-containing LBE environments remains unclear. The high solubility of the major alloying elements (Ni, Cr, Fe) of AuSSs in LBE makes LBE highly aggressive to these alloys. The microstructures of the surface and intergranular oxides can be degraded by LBE through dissolution and the oxidation would be aggravated. Currently, the microstructures of intergranular oxide in AuSSs exposed to LBE and the effect of LBE remains unclear. Systematic investigations into both the PIO microstructure and the formation mechanisms of intergranular pores and Ni-enriched phases are still needed to better understand the PIO behavior in LBE environment. The present work systematically investigated the intergranular oxidation behavior of 316L SS in oxygen-saturated LBE at 550 ◦C. Detailed microstructure characterization was conducted on the PIO fronts of two types of GB. A mechanism for intergranular oxidation of 316L SS exposed to LBE was proposed.
